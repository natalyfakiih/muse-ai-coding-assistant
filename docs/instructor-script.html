01

Why this screen exists

30 sec
DoOpen /instructor. Do not scroll yet. Let the tiles sit on screen.

So that was the student side. This is what the teacher sees.

Here is the problem it solves. You set an assignment for thirty students. Thirty submissions come back. You can see who passed the tests and who didn't, and that is genuinely all you can see. You have no idea what any of them actually understood.

Everything on this page is built from data the student generated just by working normally. Nobody filled in a survey.

02

Class at a glance

30 sec
DoPoint at the tiles left to right. Do not read all six aloud, pick the two that carry meaning.

Eight students, eighty four questions asked.

This one matters: sixty four percent of those questions were about the student's own code, not about Python. That is the ratio we want. It means they are asking "why is my loop wrong", not "what is a loop".

And four direct hints taken across the whole cohort. The hint button is always available. Almost nobody reaches for it.

If the ratio looks lowDo not defend it. Say: in a real course that number is the thing you would watch week to week. A class drifting toward factual questions is a class that stopped debugging.
03

The headline finding

60 sec
DoScroll to "Where the class diverged from its own specifications". Put your cursor on the top row and leave it there while you talk.

This is the panel I would actually open on a Monday morning.

Every difference between what a student promised and what they built, grouped by the thing it was about. And look at the top row.

— pause, let them read it —

Six of the eight students made the same mistake. They all said that a negative input should return an empty list. Their code does return an empty list. But not for the reason they gave.

In Python, range of a negative number is just empty, so the loop never runs. They got the right answer by accident. Not one of them decided it.

That is not thirty separate mistakes to mark. That is one sentence I need to say to the whole room tomorrow.

Why this landsIt is the strongest thirty seconds in the demo. Do not rush it and do not follow it with another statistic. Let it sit, then move to the categories.
04

The three kinds of difference

45 sec
DoPoint at the coloured pills in the Category column: amber, red, blue.

Not every difference means the same thing, so we sort them into three.

Drift is the amber one. The spec said one thing, the code does another, and nothing in their session shows they noticed. That is the interesting one, pedagogically.

Bug in red. The code is trying to do what they said and getting it wrong. That is ordinary. That is just programming.

Revision in blue. They changed their mind on purpose, and that is completely fine. But here is the part I am proud of: the system cannot call something a revision unless it can quote the student saying so. No quote in the conversation log, it is not a revision.

If asked why that mattersBecause otherwise the model gives everyone the benefit of the doubt. "They probably meant to" is exactly the judgement we do not want an AI making about a student's work.
05

One student, in depth

60 sec
DoScroll to the Students table. Point at the cohort column, then click Nour Z.

You can also go one student at a time.

Look at the shape of this table first. The A cohort took seven or eight drafts to write their specification and asked six to eight questions. Down here, fourteen drafts and seventeen questions. Same assignment, completely different amount of struggle, and none of it shows up in a grade.

Let me open one.

— click, then scroll the right hand column —

Here is every version of her specification, in order. You can watch her work out what the function was supposed to do. Here is every question she asked and how it was classified. And down here, each difference we found, with her own explanation of it, in her words, which she had to write before the submission was accepted.

06

The summary button

30 sec
DoClick "Generate for selected student". It takes a couple of seconds. Keep talking through it.

If you want it in prose, there is a button.

And this is the only AI call on this entire page. Everything else you have seen is a database query. Not a model deciding anything, not a summary that might be wrong. A group by.

That is deliberate. A dashboard an instructor cannot trust is worse than no dashboard, so the analysis is arithmetic and the writing is a button you press on purpose.

If it is slow or failsSay so plainly and move on: it is a free tier API and it falls back to a second provider. Then point at the provider log in beat 8. A visible failover is a better story than a hidden one.
07

The line we would not cross

35 sec
DoScroll to the dependency profile visibility panel at the bottom right.

One more thing, and it is the design decision I would defend hardest.

MUSE also builds a dependency profile: how many hints a student took, how quickly they asked for help, which topics they kept circling. Useful for a student to see about themselves.

It is student facing by default, and an instructor cannot see it unless the course explicitly opts in. And that is not a checkbox on this page. It is stored in the data model, and the API refuses the request outright without it.

Because the moment a student thinks asking for help is being counted against them, they stop asking. And then the tutor is useless.

08

The provider log

20 sec · optional
DoOnly include this for a technical audience, or if beat 6 was slow. Point at the provider log panel.

Quick engineering note. Two providers: Mistral first, Groq on failover.

Roughly half of these calls fell back, because the free Mistral tier rate limits hard. There is a circuit breaker so we stop retrying a provider that is refusing us, which took the average call from about four seconds to about one and a half.

Every call is logged. If it gets slow in front of you, this panel tells you why.

09

Close

25 sec
DoScroll back up to the divergence panel. End on the 75% row, not on a tile.

So to close the loop.

The student side makes them decide what they are building before they can type. This side turns all those decisions into the first honest answer to "what did my class actually not understand?"

Not who finished. What they misunderstood, and what I need to teach next.

QA

Questions you should expect

prep

Is this real data or did you make it up?

Say it straight: it is generated demo data, written deliberately so the panels have something to show, and the generator is in the repo. The system runs on real student sessions, and the pipeline producing these rows is the same one either way. Do not let anyone catch you implying it is a real cohort.

Couldn't a student just paste the assignment into the spec?

They could, and it would not get them far. There is a guard that drops anything the model pulled from the assignment text rather than from what the student typed. You do not get credit for an answer you never gave.

What stops the AI just giving away the answer?

Nothing, if you only ask it nicely in a prompt. So the rules are not in the prompt. The rubric check is Python, the sandbox is a subprocess with real limits, and the extraction guard is a string comparison. The model is asked for phrasing, not for permission.

Does this scale to a real class of two hundred?

The dashboard is a group by, so reading scales fine. The cost is the tutor calls during a session, and those are per student. Be honest: that is the number you would have to watch, and it is why the classification tier uses a small model.

Isn't this just making students do more work?

Yes, and that is the point, but do not say it that way. Say: it moves the work earlier. The specification is ten minutes that saves an hour of debugging something you never defined. That is the same trade a design doc makes.
